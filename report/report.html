1. Problem Definition

1.1 Context and Background

WalletCo (a hypothetical company) runs a mobile e-wallet in Vietnam with about 6 million monthly active users and 45,000 merchants. Customers top up from linked bank accounts or cards, pay merchants by QR code, transfer money peer-to-peer (P2P), pay bills and buy phone credit. The platform processes about 1.2 million transactions per day with an average value of 350,000 VND, i.e. roughly 12.6 trillion VND of GMV per month. Traffic is very uneven: lunchtime, payday, the Tet holiday and "double-day" sales (9.9, 10.10, 11.11) produce peaks of 10–20× the daily average.

Because wallet payments are instant and irreversible, a fraudulent payment cannot be "pulled back" once it is settled. The main fraud patterns are:

  • Account takeover (ATO) – stolen OTPs or SIM-swap, followed by fast transfers to mule accounts;
  • Stolen card / bank-link top-up – a fraudster links a stolen card, tops up and cashes out;
  • Social-engineering (scam) transfers – the real customer is tricked into sending money;
  • Promotion abuse – farms of fake accounts collect cash-back vouchers;
  • Merchant collusion – fake QR "sales" used to cash out stolen balances.

Fraud matters for three reasons: (1) direct financial loss, because WalletCo refunds victims; (2) regulatory exposure, since payment intermediaries licensed by the State Bank of Vietnam must manage fraud and protect customer data; and (3) trust – a customer who loses money or is wrongly blocked often leaves the platform.

1.2 Problem Statement

For every payment request, WalletCo must decide within the payment's time budget (≤ 300 ms end-to-end, of which at most 100 ms is available for fraud checks) whether to approve it, ask for step-up authentication, hold it for manual review, or block it. The current decision process has the following measured problems (last 6 months):

ProblemCurrent valueImpact
Fraud loss rate≈ 6 bps of GMV (≈ 7.5 bn VND / month)Direct loss refunded to customers; rising ~20% per quarter.
Share of fraud value stopped before settlement≈ 45%More than half of the fraud is discovered only after money has left.
False decline rate (good payments blocked or abandoned at challenge)≈ 1.2% of transactions≈ 14,000 good payments disrupted per day; complaints and churn.
Manual review queue≈ 9,000 cases / day, precision ≈ 4%25 analysts cannot keep up; median hold time 3.5 h.
Time to react to a new fraud pattern2–4 weeksNew rules are written and tested by hand.

The ML problem is a binary classification task: estimate the probability that a payment is fraudulent, P(fraud | payment, customer, device, merchant, history), and turn that probability into one of four actions with a cost-aware policy. The target is to halve the fraud loss rate while reducing false declines and analyst workload (exact targets in Section 3).

1.3 Current Situation

Fraud is handled today by a rule engine of about 180 hand-written rules (for example, "block if more than 5 transfers in 10 minutes to new recipients" or "review if top-up > 20 M VND from a card linked < 24 h ago"), plus a fixed daily limit per account. The rules are evaluated synchronously inside the Payment Service. Payments that hit a "review" rule are held and sent to a queue that 25 fraud analysts work through in a spreadsheet-like back-office tool. Confirmed fraud is learned weeks later through customer complaints and bank chargebacks.

The approach has reached its limits:

  • Rules look at one or two signals at a time and use fixed cut-offs, so they cannot combine weak signals (new device and unusual hour and new recipient and amount near the limit).
  • Fraudsters learn the cut-offs and stay just below them.
  • Every new rule adds false positives; nobody dares to delete old rules, so the false-decline rate keeps rising.
  • There is no consistent record of why a payment was stopped, which makes customer-support and audit difficult.

1.4 Why Machine Learning is the Appropriate Approach

  • Pattern complexity: fraud signals are weak individually and strong in combination; gradient-boosted trees learn these non-linear interactions from hundreds of features automatically.
  • Labelled history exists: chargebacks, disputes and analyst verdicts give ≈ 350,000 confirmed fraud labels per year among ≈ 440 million transactions – enough for supervised learning despite the 0.08% positive rate.
  • Changing adversary: fraud patterns drift monthly; a retrained model adapts in days instead of weeks of manual rule writing.
  • Graded output: a calibrated probability allows a graduated response (allow / challenge / review / block) and a cost-based threshold, which binary rules cannot provide.
  • Proven practice: ML risk scoring is standard in card networks and large payment companies, and the problem needs one model only, which keeps the system maintainable.

ML does not replace rules entirely. Regulatory and contractual constraints (sanction lists, daily limits, blocked devices) remain as hard rules evaluated before the model, and the rule engine is kept as the fallback when the model is unavailable.

Integration requirements

  • Payment Service: synchronous call with a hard timeout of 150 ms; the Payment Service owns the final authorisation.
  • Event bus (Kafka): the Payment Service, login service and device SDK publish events; the fraud system consumes them for streaming features and publishes its decisions.
  • Authentication Service: executes CHALLENGE actions (OTP, face match) and reports the outcome back as a signal.
  • Case Management tool: receives REVIEW cases with score and reasons; returns analyst verdicts as labels.
  • Data warehouse / data lake: source of historical data and destination of decision logs; dispute and chargeback files from partner banks arrive daily.
  • Customer Support CRM: read-only view of decisions and reason codes for a given transaction.

User interaction requirements

  • Customers see a short, non-revealing message (“For your safety, please confirm with OTP”) and never the score.
  • Fraud analysts see a case page with score, reason codes, recent account activity and linked accounts; they can approve, reject, or mark the account; each verdict is stored as a label.
  • Risk managers can change decision thresholds and add emergency rules through a configuration UI, with four-eyes approval and versioning – no code deployment needed.
  • ML engineers use a dashboard to compare model versions and promote or roll back a model.

Data quality

  • Label delay: a transaction is treated as "mature" only after 60 days; recent data is used for monitoring with partial labels, and training uses only mature windows (plus confirmed fraud from recent weeks).
  • Label noise: some disputes are "friendly fraud" (customer lies); unreviewed transactions are assumed legitimate. Label sources are weighted by reliability and audited monthly on a sample.
  • Class imbalance: ≈ 0.08% positives by count (≈ 0.25% by value). Handled with down-sampling of negatives and class weights; evaluation always on the original distribution.
  • Training–serving skew: features are defined once in the feature store and computed by the same code for online and offline; a daily job compares online-logged features with recomputed offline features.
  • Validation gates: schema, null rate, value ranges and distribution checks (Great Expectations) on every ingestion; a failed check stops the training pipeline.
  • Leakage control: point-in-time joins only; features that are written after a decision (e.g. "account blocked") are excluded.

Privacy

  • Processing is based on the legitimate purpose of fraud prevention stated in the terms of service and privacy notice, in line with Vietnam's Decree 13/2023/ND-CP on personal data protection.
  • Data minimisation: no names, phone numbers or ID-card numbers in the feature store; user IDs are pseudonymised; card numbers are tokenised (PCI DSS scope stays outside the ML system); location is reduced to coarse cells.
  • No sensitive attributes (gender, ethnicity, religion, health) are used as features; fairness is checked by comparing false-decline rates across regions, age bands and account tenure.
  • Encryption in transit and at rest; access to raw data only for named roles and logged; retention 5 years for audit data, 24 months for model-training feature tables.

Operating point. Thresholds are not fixed by hand. For each action the policy chooses the threshold that minimises expected cost: cost = P(fraud) × amount × loss-share − (1 − P(fraud)) × friction cost, where friction cost is the estimated revenue and churn cost of interrupting a good customer (≈ 2% abandonment for a challenge, ≈ 15% for a block). The cut-offs are then capped so that the review queue stays within analyst capacity.

4. High-level Architecture Design

4.1 Architecture Overview

The system has three planes that share one feature definition layer: an online plane that scores each payment synchronously, a streaming plane that keeps behavioural features fresh, and an offline plane that builds training data, trains, evaluates and releases models. Monitoring and the analyst feedback loop connect the planes.

4.2 Data Flow

A. Online scoring path (one payment)

  1. The customer confirms a payment in the app; the Payment Service validates balance and limits and calls the Fraud Scoring API with the transaction payload (timeout 150 ms).
  2. The API fetches ≈ 120 pre-computed features for the user, device, recipient and merchant from the online feature store in one batched Redis call (≈ 5 ms) and adds request-time features (amount ÷ user's 30-day median, hour of day, is-new-recipient).
  3. The LightGBM model (loaded in-process) returns a calibrated probability (≈ 3 ms); TreeSHAP computes the top contributing features, which are mapped to reason codes.
  4. The Decision Policy first applies hard rules, then compares the score with amount-dependent thresholds and returns ALLOW / CHALLENGE / REVIEW / BLOCK with reason codes.
  5. The Payment Service executes the action (calls the Authentication Service for CHALLENGE, holds funds for REVIEW) and publishes the payment event and the decision record to Kafka.

B. Streaming feature path

  1. Flink consumes payment, login and device events, keyed by user, device and recipient, and maintains sliding-window aggregates (count and sum of transfers in 10 min / 1 h / 24 h, distinct recipients, failed-OTP count, new-device flag).
  2. Updated aggregates are upserted into Redis within seconds, so the next payment of a fast-moving fraudster already sees them.

C. Offline learning and feedback path

  1. A Kafka sink writes all events and decision logs to the data lake, partitioned by day. Labels from analyst verdicts, disputes and partner-bank chargeback files are loaded daily.
  2. Airflow runs the training pipeline weekly: validate → build features → point-in-time label join → train → evaluate. The candidate model and its metrics are logged to MLflow and registered.
  3. A candidate that passes the evaluation gate is promoted through shadow → canary → production. Monitoring compares live behaviour with the training baseline and can trigger an early retrain.

5. Trade-off Analysis

Five significant trade-offs shaped the design. For each, the options, the analysis and the decision are given.

5.1 Accuracy vs. Latency

Options. (a) Logistic regression – < 1 ms, PR-AUC ≈ 0.38; (b) LightGBM with ≈ 500 trees – ≈ 3 ms, PR-AUC ≈ 0.58 (expected); (c) a sequence model (Transformer over the last 100 events) or a graph neural network over the transfer graph – possibly +0.03–0.05 PR-AUC but 30–80 ms on GPU plus heavier feature retrieval.

Analysis. The fraud check has at most 100 ms of the payment's 300 ms budget, and feature retrieval plus network already use ≈ 20–30 ms. A deep model would consume most of the remaining budget at p99, need GPU serving and make explanations harder. Most of the signal that a sequence or graph model would learn can be approximated with engineered velocity and counter-party features computed by Flink.

Decision. LightGBM with engineered streaming features. Graph signals (e.g. "recipient is two hops from a known mule") are pre-computed offline every hour and served as features, moving the expensive part out of the request path. A deep model may be revisited as an asynchronous second-stage scorer for REVIEW cases only.

5.2 Freshness vs. Cost

Options. (a) Daily batch features only; (b) streaming features for fast-changing signals plus batch for slow ones; (c) everything streaming plus continuous (online) model training.

Analysis. ATO attacks empty an account in minutes: a "transfers in the last 10 minutes" feature that is a day old is useless. On the other hand, merchant dispute rates or account tenure change slowly and do not justify a streaming job. Continuous training is expensive, hard to validate, and can be poisoned by fraudsters; labels arrive with days or weeks of delay anyway, so the model weights cannot be truly "real-time".

Decision. Hybrid (b): ≈ 25 velocity and session features via Flink (≤ 5 s lag), the rest computed daily; model retrained weekly, not continuously, with emergency retraining on drift. This gives most of the freshness benefit for roughly a third of the cost of an all-streaming design.

5.3 Simplicity vs. Performance

Options. (a) One global model; (b) separate models per transaction type (P2P, QR, top-up, withdrawal); (c) a global model plus a stacked ensemble.

Analysis. Per-type models could fit each fraud pattern better, but they multiply training pipelines, monitoring dashboards, thresholds and on-call complexity by four, and the smaller types would have too few positive labels. The assignment's guidance and the team size (3 data scientists, 2 ML engineers) both favour one model.

Decision. One global LightGBM with transaction type as a feature and per-type thresholds in the decision policy. Segment metrics are monitored; a separate model would be introduced only if one segment stays below its target for two consecutive months.

5.4 Automation vs. Control

Options. (a) The model decides everything automatically; (b) every flagged payment is reviewed by a human; (c) graduated automation with human oversight.

Analysis. Full automation is fast but can block many good customers when the model misbehaves (e.g. after a data bug), and regulators expect human accountability for significant decisions. Full human review is impossible at 1.2 M payments per day and adds hours of delay.

Decision. Graduated automation (c): low risk → ALLOW automatically; medium → CHALLENGE (the customer, not an analyst, resolves it); high-value medium-high → REVIEW by an analyst; very high → BLOCK, with an easy appeal through customer support. Humans control the policy (thresholds, hard rules) through a reviewed config, model releases need an approval in MLflow, and automatic guards roll back a model if the decision mix moves outside its normal band.

5.5 Privacy vs. Personalisation

Options. (a) Use every available signal, including contacts, precise GPS and installed apps; (b) use only transaction-related and coarse device data; (c) (b) plus privacy-preserving extras with consent.

Analysis. Rich personal data would improve the model a little, but it conflicts with data-minimisation under Decree 13/2023/ND-CP, increases the impact of a breach, and erodes customer trust. Experience in fraud detection shows that most of the predictive power comes from behaviour (velocity, device, counter-party), not from personal attributes.

Decision. (b) with pseudonymised IDs, coarse location cells and no sensitive attributes. Optional signals (telco SIM-swap check) are used only with consent and a contract. Fairness metrics per segment are reported monthly to Compliance.

5.6 Summary

6. Risks and Rollout Plan

RiskMitigation
Fraudsters adapt to the modelWeekly retraining; monitoring of new-pattern clusters; analysts can add emergency rules without a deployment.
Feedback loop bias (blocked payments never get labels)Keep a small random "control" sample (0.5% of low-value flagged payments are challenged instead of blocked) to observe outcomes; use challenge results as weak labels.
Training–serving skewShared feature definitions in Feast; daily parity check; shadow period before every release.
Outage of Redis, Flink or the scorerTimeouts, circuit breaker and fallback to the rule engine; multi-AZ deployment; runbooks and on-call.
Unfair impact on a customer segmentNo sensitive features; monthly segment report; threshold caps per segment.

Rollout (≈ 6 months). Month 1–2: data pipelines, feature store, offline baseline. Month 3: first model, shadow mode next to the rule engine. Month 4: canary on 10% of P2P transfers, then all P2P. Month 5: all transaction types; analyst UI with reasons. Month 6: retire overlapping rules, review results against the targets in Section 3.5.

7. Conclusion

The proposed design replaces a saturated rule engine with a single, explainable LightGBM risk model fed by streaming and batch features, and a cost-aware decision policy that turns scores into graduated actions. It fits within the 100 ms latency budget, degrades safely to the existing rules, and keeps humans in control of policy and releases. The goal hierarchy ties every model metric to a system and business outcome: halving the fraud loss rate (6 → 3 bps of GMV), cutting false declines from 1.2% to 0.5% and shrinking the review queue by two thirds. The chosen trade-offs favour simplicity, freshness where it matters, and privacy, leaving clear upgrade paths (graph features, a second-stage model) once the base system has proven its value in production.
